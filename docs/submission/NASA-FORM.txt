PULSAR

================================================================================
HIGH-LEVEL PROJECT SUMMARY
================================================================================

PULSAR is health-monitoring software for a crew on a long mission. A watch and guided kits collect indicators. Each astronaut sees only their own data, in plain language, with the action to take. The crew medical officer sees the whole crew, validates or replaces the proposed action, and can run a six-step guided ultrasound. Flight surgeons in Houston receive a short report at each communication window and queue a recommendation back to the vehicle. Heart rate is forecast with XGBoost, an LSTM, and Prophet on a demonstration series. Space-weather notices come from NASA DONKI and stay on the ship server. This is decision support, not a diagnosis. Crew vital signs on screen are simulated, because real astronaut medical records are private.

DETAILED PROJECT DESCRIPTION
================================================================================

What it does

PULSAR follows one chain: watch, astronaut tablet, crew medical officer, Houston.

The astronaut opens one personal dashboard (identifier AST-). Dials show resting heart rate, SpO2, temperature, blood pressure, and sleep. A 3D body map marks the zone that needs attention. A to-do list leads to the next measurement. Forecasts compare an observed line with a dashed prediction over 24 hours or 7 days, and state the time until a threshold. Proposed actions are concrete and non-diagnostic: hydrate, rest, repeat the measurement, shelter from radiation, or call the crew medical officer. The astronaut can apply an action, mark it done, or escalate it.

Guided kits record glucose (fasting or after a meal), blood pressure, temperature, and mood (1 to 5). Each card shows a Nominal, Caution, or Warning lamp with an icon and a word, the action to take, and who is notified. A face scan uses the tablet camera only to frame the face. Fatigue numbers on that screen are simulated. The image is not saved and is not sent.

The crew medical officer (CMO-) sees every crew member, ordered by severity, with a file, live charts, a latest-value table, model alerts, and notes. Ultrasound is six locked steps: connect the probe, choose the zone, place the probe, view the image, annotate, attach and send. If the link is down, the report waits for the next window.

Houston (NASA-FS-) reads the reports, the crew table, the forecast summary, and any attached ultrasound stills, then queues a recommendation (routine, priority, or urgent) for the next uplink. The interface states the radio delay: near zero in low Earth orbit, about 1.3 seconds at the Moon, 4 to 24 minutes at Mars.

How it works

The browser is a React, TypeScript, and Vite application. It calls a FastAPI ship server. If the server does not answer, a local simulator takes over and the screen shows a Simulator chip and a DEMO tag. The server retries every 30 seconds.

Heart-rate forecasts are computed on the server by three models written in Python from the published methods: second-order boosting (XGBoost), a one-layer LSTM trained by backpropagation through time, and an additive trend plus Fourier seasonality (Prophet). They run on a demonstration heart-rate series. The other indicators stay a scripted scenario. Every forecast screen says this is decision support, not a diagnosis.

Thresholds are cabin watch points taken from published Earth references and written in the requirements: resting heart rate caution at 100 bpm and alert at 120; fasting glucose 70–99 mg/dL, alert at 126 or below 54; systolic pressure caution at 130 mmHg and alert at 160. They are not flight limits. In microgravity a flight surgeon would replace them with each astronaut’s pre-flight baseline. The lamp opens the review. The decision uses the personal trend.

Alarms follow the idea of IEC 60601-1-8. Only a red alert makes a sound: one beep, always with a visual banner. It cannot be silenced. It can be acknowledged, or paused for two minutes after it has been seen. Orange alerts are visual only.

A cooperative regularity layer (measurements done, routines followed) can be switched off. It never uses a health value and never ranks crew members.

Benefits and what we hope to achieve

On a Mars transit, Houston’s answer can arrive after the crew has already acted. PULSAR gives the astronaut a plain next step, gives the crew medical officer a validated path, and gives Houston a short report instead of a raw stream. The next step, with a flight surgeon, is to replace the demonstration series and the Earth thresholds with personal baselines.

Tools

Frontend: React 19, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS, TanStack Query, Recharts, three.js, lucide icons. Backend: Python, FastAPI. Watch path: ESP32-C3 over Wi-Fi, with a buzzer for the red alert. Heart-rate models: pure Python implementations of XGBoost, LSTM, and Prophet. No official XGBoost, PyTorch, or Prophet packages are installed.

================================================================================
NASA DATA
================================================================================

1. NASA DONKI Notifications API
https://api.nasa.gov/DONKI/notifications
The ship server requests the last 7 days, all notification types, with a 3-second timeout. The API key stays in the server environment and never reaches the browser. The result is shown on the astronaut watch screen and on the crew-surgeon board as a space-weather card. Provenance is Connected, Cache, or Offline. The card is labeled as a NASA reference, not a crew measurement. It tells the crew when a solar notice is active, so a radiation-shelter action can be tied to a real event. If the call fails, the server serves the last cache, then a local file.

2. NASA Human Research Program hazards
https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/
The five hazards (space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, altered gravity, hostile closed environment) organize the public home page, the 3D body map, the ultrasound zones (heart, eye for SANS, lungs, kidneys, thigh muscle), and the daily routines. This is a NASA knowledge framework, not a personal health dataset.

3. What is not NASA medical data
Individual astronaut health records are privacy-protected and were not used. Heart rate, SpO2, blood pressure, glucose, temperature, sleep, and mood on screen are a fictional demonstration crew. Threshold numbers are cited Earth references (CDC, AHA), listed under References, and are not flight medical limits.
